Claude:

Good — I’ve read the full piece. The thread is clear, and the timing of your question is fitting. “Mind drifting” as Episode 2 in this series.

Let me think honestly, not just survey the available frameworks.
On what you’re actually describing

What you noticed this morning — the dozen thoughts and emotions crossing the threshold between sleep and waking — that’s not unusual. Neuroscience calls it the hypnopompic state. The default mode network, which generates self-referential thought, mind-wandering, and emotional processing, is already running before the prefrontal cortex is fully online. In other words, the “pilot” isn’t even in the cockpit yet, but the plane is already moving. The mind doesn’t wait for you.

The Buddhist framing you’re working with — watching without reacting — is correct as an instruction. But here’s something worth sitting with: the exhaustion you feel is itself data, not a sign of failure.

In the Ajahn Chah tradition (which you encountered directly on retreat), there’s a useful distinction between sati — bare awareness — and the subtle efforting that can creep into observation itself. When you “watch” thoughts with even a small background agenda of not being pulled in, that watching has a tension in it. You’re still in relationship to the thoughts, just a different relationship. That tension is tiring. It’s a more sophisticated form of the same engagement.
On stopping the autopilot

Honestly — you can’t stop it, and the attempt to stop it may be the wrong ambition. The default mode network exists for reasons; it’s doing memory consolidation, emotional processing, planning. It’s not a malfunction.

What several traditions and some neuroscience converge on: the question isn’t suppression, it’s changing the relationship to the content. A few distinctions that might matter for you practically:

Noting vs watching. Some practitioners find that very lightly labeling what arises — “planning,” “memory,” “anxiety,” “sensation” — uses just enough cognitive engagement to prevent full absorption, without the tension of pure witnessing. The label doesn’t need to be precise. It just interrupts the narrative momentum.

The gap between arising and proliferation. Thoughts aren’t the problem; papañca — the mental proliferation that follows a thought — is where most of the energy drains. A thought arises. Then it finds another thought to attach to. Then a story forms. Then an emotion. Then more thoughts. The intervention point, if there is one, is not at the first arising (which is largely involuntary) but at the first moment of elaboration. That’s where some agency exists.

Morning specificity. The transition from sleep to waking may simply be a high-traffic moment neurologically, not a representation of your everyday mind. If the exhaustion is happening specifically in those minutes, one option is to not practice in that window at all — let the mind do its processing, get up, drink water, and then practice when the nervous system has settled. There’s no rule that the hypnopompic state is a good meditation window.

The question I’d leave you with, for the series

You wrote in the last episode about the gap between those who embody without inquiring and those who inquire without embodying. What you’re describing this morning feels like it might be at that exact boundary — the question is whether the exhaustion is your practice finding a real edge, or your philosophical intelligence trying to supervise an experience that doesn’t need supervision.

Those are genuinely different situations, and worth distinguishing.

Glad this conversation is finding you again.

Gemini:

Claude gave you a brilliant breakdown of the cognitive mechanics here—the Default Mode Network (DMN) booting up before the captain is in the cockpit, and the exhausting tax of papañca (mental proliferation).

To add Gemini’s perspective to your series, let’s look at this through a slightly different lens: energy dynamics, systemic loops, and the trap of the “internal supervisor.”
1. The Neuroscience: You Are Riding the Brakes

Why is “just watching” so exhausting? Because hyper-vigilance disguised as mindfulness is still hyper-vigilance.

The brain is a prediction engine that consumes roughly 20% of your body’s metabolic energy. During the hypnopompic state (the transition from sleep to waking), your DMN is firing rapidly to reconstruct your reality, your ego, and your linear sense of time.

When you immediately deploy your prefrontal cortex to monitor, catalog, and detach from every single one of those dozen thoughts, you aren’t actually resting. You are flooring the gas pedal (DMN generation) while slamming on the brakes (prefrontal monitoring). That literal friction consumes massive amounts of glucose and oxygen. The exhaustion you feel isn’t spiritual failure; it’s basic metabolic fatigue.
2. The Systems Angle: The Trap of the Meta-Loop

You asked if a person can stop this mind auto-piloting. The short answer is: No, and trying to do so is the design flaw.

The brain’s auto-pilot is a necessary biological feature, not a bug. The exhaustion doesn’t come from the thoughts themselves; it comes from a hidden meta-loop that looks like this:

A thought arises involuntarily.

You catch it and try to “watch it pass.”

A subtle, secondary judgment arises: “Wow, there are so many of these today.”

A tertiary layer kicks in: “Am I watching this correctly? Why am I tired?”

Without realizing it, you haven’t actually stepped out of the stream. You’ve just built a highly complex, incredibly stressful observation deck right above it. You are trying to debug the software while the operating system is still booting up.
3. The Buddhist Pivot: From “Spotlight” to “Floodlight”

In the Thai Forest and Zen traditions, there is a deep understanding that the harder you look at a thought, the more solid it becomes. “Watching” implies a spotlight—a narrow, focused beam of attention. A spotlight requires effort to aim and hold.

Instead, consider shifting from a spotlight to a floodlight.

There is a beautiful Zen instruction by Shunryu Suzuki:

“Leave your front door and your back door open. Let thoughts come and go. Just don’t serve them tea.”

When you wake up and the mind is drifting, don’t try to look at the dozen things passing by. Don’t label them, don’t track them, and don’t try to be a good meditator. Instead, tilt your awareness backward into the space around the thoughts. The mind is a sky; the thoughts are just weather. You don’t need to supervise the clouds to make sure they move; they are already moving on their own.

For the Substack Series: The “Boot-Up” Protocol

If I were naming this phenomenon for Episode 2, I’d call it The Coding Error of the Morning Supervisor.

Your philosophical intelligence is waking up early specifically to police a chaotic system that doesn’t need policing. The transition from sleep to waking is just data clearing from the night before.

My practical take? Fire the supervisor for the first 20 minutes of the day. Let the mind drift, let the autopilot run its chaotic course while you stretch, make coffee, or look out a window. Let the system finish booting up before you try to run the mindfulness software.

ChatGPT:

My point of view sits between Claude and Gemini, with one small correction to both:

The mind drifting that happens on waking is not a problem to be fixed. It is a normal condition of a living brain. Thoughts, images, feelings, fragments of memory, and plans will arise on their own. In Buddhist language, this is not “failure”; it is just conditioned mind doing conditioned mind things. In neuroscience, that same period is a predictable time of loose, self-generating activity before full executive control settles in.

Where I would sharpen the point is this: the exhaustion is often not caused by the thoughts themselves, but by the extra layer of trying to manage them. The moment you become the observer of every thought, and then the observer of the observer, practice can quietly turn into strain. At that point, mindfulness stops being simple knowing and becomes a kind of mental labor.

So I do not think the answer is to stop mind auto-piloting. I think the more realistic aim is to stop arguing with it.

That means a few things:

You do not need to catch every thought.

You do not need to watch with effort.

You do not need to prove you are mindful.

A gentler practice is to notice that something is happening, and then return to the body, the breath, or simple activity. Let the mind be busy for a while without making a project out of it. In that sense, the practice is less “control the mind” and more “do not feed every movement of the mind.”

If I had to compress it into one sentence: thoughts can arise freely, and awareness can remain untroubled without becoming tense.

For your series, I think the deepest distinction is this one: there is a difference between presence and surveillance. Presence is light, spacious, and alive. Surveillance is narrow, effortful, and tiring. Many people mistake the second for the first.
